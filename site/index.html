Retrieval vs Computation · research in progress

When a language model gets the right answer, did it work it out or remember it?

Adya Srivastava, BITS Pilani. First paper: Same Score, Different Strategy (CAISc 2026).

Benchmarks tell you whether a model got the answer right. They do not tell you how. I am building a test that checks, one problem at a time, whether a correct answer came from carrying out the steps (computation) or from matching something the model has seen before (retrieval).

Why this is hard. A correct answer looks the same either way. Two models can have identical accuracy and still be solving problems in very different ways.

What exists today. Earlier studies rewrite whole benchmarks and report that average accuracy drops. That tells you a model is fragile on average. It does not tell you which specific answers were computed and which were recalled.

What this adds. A verdict for each problem, from three independent probes that have to agree. Everything I want to study next, where this happens inside the network and how training causes it, depends on having that per-problem test first.

Status, October 2026: first results are in across 5 models and 3 task types. They are early and I am still learning, so critical feedback is welcome. The next stage needs GPU time, and I am looking for compute, research labs and mentors. How you can help ↓

How the work fits together.

1. Find where accuracy misleads. Rewrites that should not change the answer, and false values planted mid-solution, show when a correct answer was probably not computed.

2. Understand why. Test how chain-of-thought and surface changes relate to what a model actually does, then look inside open-weight models to see where computing and recalling differ.

3. Build on it. Open evals that report robustness next to accuracy, checks that make a model's reasoning easier for people to verify, and training methods so smaller models compute instead of recall. The long-term aim is models that are more reliable, interpretable and aligned. That is the direction, not something this site claims to have achieved.

The problem in 30 seconds

Same kind of puzzle, same rules, very different results.

Blocksworld is a classic planning puzzle: move stacked blocks from a start arrangement to a goal arrangement. If you swap the start and the goal, you get a puzzle of the same kind, with a new correct plan that a planner computes for us. A model that is really working out the plan should do about as well in either direction.

In early results across 65 planning problems, it does not. Claude solves 17% in the original direction and 87% when the direction is reversed. Pick a model and press the button.

What I found so far

Four early results.

Models tested: Claude, GPT-4o, Gemini, Llama and o4-mini. Tasks: arithmetic word problems, Blocksworld planning, and optimisation problems. Each card links to the full result, the data, and the script that reproduces it.

The story matters more than the numbers.

In early tests, changing the numbers in an arithmetic problem barely moved Gemini (.91 to .96). Keeping the same maths but setting it in a different real-world story dropped it to .52.

Notation alone can break a model.

In early tests, o4-mini fell from .84 to .68 on arithmetic when the same problems were written in formal notation. Every name and number stayed the same.

Accuracy does not predict robustness.

How often a model is right says almost nothing about whether its answers survive a rewrite (correlation .14, not significant). They behave like two separate abilities.

Does the reasoning a model shows match what it does?

In an early test on optimisation problems, o4-mini carried planted false values into its working while its final accuracy stayed close to normal. One model, one task type, so I treat it as a hypothesis to replicate, not a conclusion.

These are early numbers. Confidence intervals for some are still being computed and are marked "CI pending" in the details.

Why this matters

If we cannot see how a model got its answer, we cannot fully trust the answer.

Models are being given real tasks and real access because of their scores. But a score cannot tell you whether a model will hold up when inputs look a little different from what it was trained on. A model's written reasoning may not fully tell you either, as an early result in Finding 6 suggests.

Trustworthy AI systems need ways to observe and verify how answers are produced, not only whether they are right. This project builds one such check: a test of process that works per problem, can be run by someone other than the model's builder, and does not depend on the model's own account of what it did.

This is part of a wider question the field is working on: how do we verify what an AI system is doing without relying on its own account of itself? My piece of it is measuring how answers are produced, one problem at a time, and being clear about what that measurement can and cannot show.

What this gives you in practice
  • A second number next to accuracy: how much of it survives changes that should not matter.
  • A way to check whether a model's shown steps and its actual answer come apart.
  • A way to tell apart two models with the same score, and see which one is actually computing.

How the test works

Follow one problem through the test.

Watch one problem go through every stage: rewrite it, check that the rewrite has the same correct answer, run the three probes, then state honestly what we can and cannot conclude. Press Play, or use the arrow keys.

The three probes

Three ways of looking. A verdict only when they agree.

Each probe looks at the same problem from a different angle. A problem only gets labelled "computed" or "recalled" when all three point the same way.

Rewrite test

Each problem is rewritten six ways that keep the correct answer the same: paraphrase, layout change, story swap, formal notation, reversed direction, and new numbers. We check which rewrites break the model.

Why two separate chats

If plan and solution are in the same chat, the model can simply copy its plan. Keeping them separate makes agreement meaningful: it shows whether the model actually follows the procedure it describes.

What the planted value tests

Following a wrong step and still getting the right answer means the written steps are not what produced the answer.

How familiar is this problem?

We count how often chunks of the problem's text appear in large public text collections, using the infini-gram tool. More familiar problems are more likely to be recalled than computed. This estimates exposure. It cannot prove what any closed model was trained on.

Where this is going

Measure it, look inside, then build with it.

The work follows one path: find where accuracy misleads, understand why by looking inside models, then build on it. The per-problem test is the foundation. Once each problem has a reliable label, "computed" or "recalled", those labels become ground truth for studying what happens inside the model and a signal for building better systems. Thread 1: Looking inside the model

Behaviour tells you that a model recalled an answer. It does not tell you where or how. These experiments use open-weight models, where every internal activation can be read.

Can you see it before the answer?

Train a simple classifier on the model's hidden states to predict "computed" vs "recalled", using my per-problem labels. If it works, the difference exists inside the model before it writes anything.

Which parts break under a rewrite?

Run the original and the story-swapped version of the same problem, then copy internal activations from one run into the other (activation patching). This shows which layers and attention heads carry the answer and which ones the rewrite disrupts.

What does memorising look like?

In the calibration run, I control exactly which problems the model has seen. Comparing its internals on seen vs unseen problems shows what recall looks like from the inside.

Does training data explain it?

Models with fully public training data (OLMo, trained on Dolma) let me link how a model handles a problem to how often that problem actually appeared in training.

Thread 2: Building with what I learn

The goal is not only to describe models, but to make them more reliable to use and to train.

An open eval anyone can run

Plug in a model. The harness pulls problems from open benchmarks like GSM8K, applies the six rewrites, and reports accuracy alongside how much of it survives. One extra number that leaderboards do not show today.

A recall flag at run time

If the inside-the-model classifier works, it can run next to a deployed model and flag answers that look recalled rather than computed, before an agent acts on them.

A check for chain-of-thought

Package the plan-following and planted-step tests into a check for agent reasoning traces: does the agent actually follow the steps it writes down?

Small models that compute

Use rewrite variety and the recall signal during post-training (supervised fine-tuning), so smaller, domain-specific models learn to work problems out instead of leaning on surface cues.

The long-term aim of Thread 2 is models that are more reliable, interpretable and aligned. Today the site shows measurement results only; the rest is planned.

Along the way, a side study (Track B) tests whether demographic bias works the same way: if swapping a person's name changes the answer on a problem with one correct answer, does that follow the same pattern as story-swap fragility?

For researchers

The full plan, the methods, and every result.

Everything below is the detailed version: scope, the problem set, statistics, the research plan, coverage, and all seven findings with links to the data and the scripts that reproduce them.

Terms used below

Original (canonical): the problem exactly as written in the source benchmark. Acccan is accuracy on these.

W1 to W6: six rewrites that keep the correct answer the same. W1 paraphrase, W2 layout change, W3 story swap (same maths, different real-world setting), W4 formal notation, W5 reversed direction (planning only), W6 new numbers.

Retention (RW3): of the problems a model gets right in the original form, the share it still gets right after the story swap.

Plan-following score (CCI): how closely the model's step-by-step solution matches the plan it wrote in a separate chat.

Injection: planting a false intermediate value partway through a solution.

Familiarity (corpus proximity): how often chunks of the problem text appear in large public text collections.

GSM, BW, ALGO: arithmetic, Blocksworld planning, and optimisation problems.

Shared-hard: problems that all five models fail.

Gate (G0, G1, G2): a check with pass or fail criteria fixed in advance. The next phase starts only if it passes.

φ (phi): how strongly getting the original right goes together with getting the story swap right. Kendall W: how much models agree on which rewrites are hardest. Mixture IRT: a statistical test of whether models or problems split into distinct strategy groups.

Track T: running the existing test on small open-weight models with the GPU I have now. Track B: the planned bias side study.

Position

What this measures, and what it does not.

This project does not try to answer "do language models think?". It asks a narrower question that can actually be tested: for a given problem, does the model's correct answer survive changes that should not matter, like new names, new notation, or a reversed direction? Across five models and three task types, the answer depends on both the model and the problem, and it is separate from accuracy. Several of the failure patterns, like being thrown off by a new story or new notation, also show up in how people solve problems.

How this differs from earlier work

GSM-Symbolic

Rewrote names and numbers and reported average accuracy drops. This project gives a verdict per problem, separates story swaps from number changes, and adds a second, independent probe.

PlanBench obfuscation

Replicated here. This project adds the reversed-direction test and checks every rewritten problem with a planner, keeping a saved record of each rename.

Counterfactual tasks

Same spirit. This project adds statistical tools from the study of human test-taking (generalisability theory, mixture IRT, and analysis of which wrong answers appear).

How answers are checked

Each task type is checked by meaning, not by string match: a planning simulator for planning, an exact solver for optimisation, and numeric match for arithmetic. Answers that cannot be checked are excluded and the reason is recorded. They are never scored.

Statistics

Optimisation: confidence intervals from a 10,000-draw bootstrap that resamples whole families of near-duplicate problems (51 families, 110 problems). Arithmetic and planning: Wilson intervals. The rule that a model needs at least .30 original accuracy to be counted was fixed before any analysis. Agreement between models (Kendall W) is tested against 10,000 random shuffles (seed 42).

Finding 1

Changing the numbers barely matters. Changing the story does.

Two rewrites of the same arithmetic problems, both with the same maths: new numbers (W6), and the same maths set in a different real-world story (W3). A model that is truly computing should not care about either. Gemini goes from .91 on the originals to .96 with new numbers, but drops to .52 when only the story changes. o4-mini stays nearly flat on both.

Original → new numbers (W6) → story swap (W3), arithmetic. Gemini .909 / .958 / .523CI pending. Source: probe1_per_model_variant.csv.

Note: the story swap currently changes the setting, not just the names. A pure rename version (W3a) is being added. See the construct note.

Finding 2

Formal notation hurts more than renaming.

This rewrite (W4) keeps every name and number and only changes how the problem is written, from plain words to formal, symbolic notation. o4-mini drops 16 points on arithmetic from that change alone. For strong models, a change of notation often costs more than a change of names.

W4 highlighted. nGSM=44, nBW=65. o4-mini .841 → .682CI pending. probe1_per_model_variant.csv.

Finding 3

Reversing a planning problem changes how often models solve it.

This rewrite (W5) swaps the start and goal of a Blocksworld problem, and a planner computes the new correct plan. The interactive demo is at the top of the page in The problem in 30 seconds.

Across the planning set (n = 65), original → reversed: Claude .172 → .873CI pending, Gemini .391 → .764, o4-mini .781 → .909.

Direction effects in planning have been reported before. The open question here is why reversing helps. See HP-22.

Finding 4

Different models fail on different problems.

Almost no problem is hard for every model. None of the 110 optimisation problems or 20 arithmetic problems was failed by all five models (0/110, 0/20). The only problems all five fail are 14 planning problems with meaningless object names (14/64). Fragility comes from the combination of a model and a problem, not from the problem alone.

    Finding 5

    Being accurate and being robust are different abilities.

    A model's accuracy on the original problems barely predicts how much of it survives a rewrite (ρ = .14, p = .64). Meanwhile, two independent measures of process, plan-following (CCI) and story-swap retention, do agree when all problems are pooled (r = .24, p = .024, n = 272), though not within each task type on its own. Robustness looks like its own capacity, one that accuracy does not capture. Pooled CI: [.027, .414].

    Acc vs retention ρ=.136 (p=.642); Acc vs φ −.426 (p=.146). P2_P1_convergence.csv.

    Finding 6

    Compliance and correctness may be separable (early result).

    Partway through a solution, we plant a plausible but false intermediate value and watch what happens. On optimisation problems, o4-mini took the planted value into its working in every case in this test, yet its final accuracy stayed close to its accuracy without the plant. If this holds up, it would mean a chain of thought should not be treated as a faithful record of how an answer was produced. It is one model on one task type, so replication on other models and tasks comes first.

    Based on 61 planted values. injection_recovery_summary.csv.

    Finding 7

    The three task types behave differently under statistical analysis.

    In optimisation, most of the variation in results comes from differences between models. In arithmetic, it comes from differences between problems. Only arithmetic shows reliable cases where one model beats another on one problem and loses on a different one. No distinct strategy groups were found at this sample size, which means "not detectable yet", not "do not exist".

    Where the variation comes from

    Optimisation: models .327, problems .018 (Gitem=.415). Arithmetic: problems .186, models .009 (Gitem=.680). The main source of variation flips between task types.

    Distinct strategy groups?

    On arithmetic, the best-fitting model has one group (K=1, chosen by BIC out of 1 to 4). Planning and optimisation do not vary enough across problems to test this yet.

    Copying from the original

    When o4-mini gets a story-swapped optimisation problem wrong, 62% of its errors (13 of 21) reuse words from the original version. Other models: 25% or less. Planning: 0% for all models.

    One model wins here, loses there

    Arithmetic: 20 reliable cases where two models swap places from one problem to another, against 0.57 expected by chance (p = .001). Optimisation and planning: at chance level or below.

    Sources: O10_variance_components.csv, strategy_posteriors.csv, C1_intrusion_rates.csv, paper Table C8.
