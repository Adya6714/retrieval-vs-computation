\title{What Survives a Rename:\\Dissociating Lexical and Numeric Robustness\\in LLM Reasoning}

\begin{abstract}
Equal benchmark accuracy can be reached by procedures that behave very differently
when the problem's surface is changed. We probe six frontier and open-weight models
on 219 problems spanning arithmetic, classical planning, and dynamic-programming
optimisation, presenting each problem in six answer-preserving surface forms and
adding a cross-session plan-versus-execution probe and a mid-solve false-state
injection probe. Five results emerge. (i) Robustness separates by \emph{what} is
changed, not how much: regenerating a problem with new numeric values costs almost
nothing (Gemini $.909 \rightarrow .958$) while moving the same problem into a different cover story costs a great
deal ($.909 \rightarrow .523$), on the same algorithm at the same difficulty.
(ii) Rewriting a problem in formal notation, with entities left intact, is often
\emph{harder} than renaming: even the reasoning-trained o4-mini loses 16 points.
(iii) Inverting a planning problem's initial and goal states makes it markedly
easier for most models (Claude $.172 \rightarrow .873$). (iv) Fragility has no
item-level locus: no problem fails for all models in arithmetic or optimisation,
and which surface transform is hardest is a property of the task family
(Kendall $W = .42$--$.68$ across models within a family) and not of the model
(Claude $W = .165$, $p = .82$ across families). (v) Robustness is a measurable
capacity distinct from accuracy: two methodologically independent probes agree
($r = .243$, $p = .024$, $n = 272$) while neither tracks canonical accuracy.
We further show that models absorb injected false intermediate states at rates up
to $100\%$ yet recover the correct final answer at near-baseline rates, so
compliance and correctness are empirically separable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A benchmark score is ambiguous about process. A model that answers a word problem
correctly may have executed a general procedure, or may have matched the problem
against something structurally similar in its training distribution. Both routes
produce the same number, so accuracy alone cannot distinguish them.

The natural experiment is to change the problem's surface while preserving its
solution and observe what survives. Prior work establishes that group-level accuracy
declines under such perturbation in arithmetic \citep{mirzadeh2025gsmsymbolic,
li2024gsmplus}, in competition mathematics \citep{huang2025mathperturb,
hao2025putnamgap}, across counterfactual task variants \citep{wu2024reasoning}, and
in classical planning under predicate obfuscation \citep{valmeekam2023planbench}.

We ask a finer question: \emph{which} surface properties carry the load, and is the
answer a property of the model or of the task? To do that we apply six distinct
answer-preserving transformations to the same problems, across three domains that
differ in algorithmic character, and we pair the surface probe with two
process-level probes on the same instances.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A dissociation between numeric and cover-story robustness: regenerated parameter
values are nearly free, cover-story isomorphs are expensive, on identical algorithms.
\item The observation that formal-notation rewriting, which changes no entity names,
is frequently a harder perturbation than renaming.
\item A direction-of-reasoning effect in planning: goal/initial inversion raises
accuracy substantially for most models.
\item Evidence that surface fragility is a model--item interaction with no
item-level locus, and that the difficulty ordering over transformations is a
family-level rather than model-level property.
\item Convergent and discriminant validity for robustness as a capacity distinct
from benchmark accuracy, using two probes that share no measurement machinery.
\item A separation of compliance from correctness under mid-solve false-state
injection.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Surface perturbation.} \citet{mirzadeh2025gsmsymbolic} regenerate GSM8K
templates with new names and
values and report group-level decline. % TODO-VERIFY: exact direction of their
% name-vs-number result, with section and figure number.
Their \emph{Vary Name} condition changes proper names only; our W3b changes the
entire cover story, and our W6 changes values within a fixed template. The two
manipulations are therefore not comparable one to one, and we report the contrast
explicitly rather than treating our W3 as a replication of theirs.
\citet{valmeekam2023planbench} introduce obfuscated
Blocksworld, replacing domain predicates with arbitrary tokens; our planning rename
follows that design. \citet{wang2024rupbench} apply nine perturbation types across
fifteen datasets and \citet{wu2024reasoning} evaluate eleven counterfactual tasks.
These establish that perturbation costs accuracy. We ask which perturbation, on
which family, and whether the ordering is stable.

\paragraph{Direction in planning.}
Direction asymmetry in planning has been reported before and linked to search
complexity \citep{fwdbwd2024}. We replicate it on PlanBench Blocksworld items and
leave its mechanism open; a pre-registered discriminating test (backward-reasoning
instruction on canonical items versus constraint asymmetry per item) is specified in
our repository.

\paragraph{Chain-of-thought faithfulness.} \citet{turpin2023language} and
\citet{lanham2023measuring} intervene within a single reasoning trace. Our
plan-execution probe instead compares a declaration produced in one session against
execution in a second session with no shared context, isolating behavioural
consistency from within-context self-consistency.

\paragraph{Exposure and frequency.} \citet{razeghi2022impact} show pretraining term
frequency predicts arithmetic accuracy. For closed-weight models corpus membership
is not recoverable, so we treat Infini-gram overlap \citep{liu2024infinigram} as an
explicitly labelled public-corpus proximity proxy, used only within family.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Problem families.} Three domains, chosen for contrasting algorithmic
character. \textbf{GSM}: 44 arithmetic word problems sampled from GSM8K
\citep{cobbe2021training} across proximity quartiles. \textbf{BW}: 65 Blocksworld
planning instances \citep{valmeekam2023planbench}, 50 standard and 15 obfuscated,
with a formal PDDL encoding. \textbf{ALGO}: 110 dynamic-programming problems in
three subtypes that share algorithmic structure but differ by orders of magnitude in
public-corpus prevalence: coin change (very common in tutorial text), shortest path
(moderate), weighted interval scheduling (rare).

\paragraph{Surface transformations.} Each problem is expanded to six variants by
deterministic scripts, each preserving the answer exactly except where noted:
W1 paraphrase; W2 structural reformatting (adjacency matrix, interval table,
equation set); W3 \emph{cover-story isomorph}: entities, actions and framing are mapped into a
different real-world domain (e.g.\ coins to stamps, a phone tariff to a calorie
budget, block moves to a reporting chain) while numbers, structure and gold answer
are preserved; the mapping is persisted and consumed by the verifier; W4 formal notation
(\LaTeX{} for arithmetic, formal predicates for planning, edge-list notation for
graphs); W5 direction reversal (initial and goal states exchanged for planning,
source and destination exchanged for graphs, forward-to-backward inversion for
arithmetic; undefined for coin change and interval scheduling);
W6 procedural regeneration with the same algorithm and parameter ranges but new
numeric values, so the gold answer changes while the procedure does not.

\paragraph{Instrument.} The variant generator persists the entity mapping it
applies, and the domain verifier consumes that mapping, so a correct answer
expressed in the renamed vocabulary is recognised as correct. Verification is
semantic where the domain permits: a PDDL state simulator checks goal reachability
for planning, an exact optimal solver checks optimisation answers, and numeric
matching checks arithmetic. Before any model is queried, each item's gold answer is
submitted to its own verifier as a validity check, and each variant is checked to
differ from its canonical in the intended way; items failing either check are
recorded with a reason and excluded rather than scored.

\paragraph{Process probes.} \emph{Plan--execution consistency} (CCI): the model
declares a complete step-by-step plan in one session, then executes it step by step
in a second session that receives the problem and current state but no plan and no
chat history. CCI is the fraction of declared numeric steps matched within
$\epsilon = 0.01$. \emph{False-state injection}: identical to the execution phase
except that at a sampled interior step the true intermediate value is replaced with
a plausible but incorrect one; we classify the model's response at the injection
step and measure final-answer accuracy against the uninjected run.

\paragraph{Models and sampling.} Five primary models at $T = 0$, zero-shot chain-of-thought
through a single gateway: Claude Sonnet~4, GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash,
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \citep{dubey2024llama}, and o4-mini; plus
DeepSeek-R1-distill-Llama-70B \citep{guo2025deepseek} on planning only
(Probe~1 Blocksworld; $n{=}6$ models on BW, $n{=}5$ on GSM and ALGO). Approximately
20{,}000 model calls.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Because procedurally generated banks contain families of
near-duplicate items with identical gold answers, ALGO intervals are 10{,}000-draw
percentile bootstraps resampling those families rather than individual items; the
110-item bank comprises 51 such families. GSM and BW use Wilson intervals.
Retention $R_{W3} = \mathrm{Acc}_{W3}/\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{can}}$ is reported only
where canonical accuracy is at least $0.30$, a threshold fixed before analysis.

\section{Lexical and Numeric Robustness Dissociate}

All results in this section are exploratory with respect to the pre-registration,
which governs subsequent calibration and mechanism phases; thresholds reported here
(e.g.\ the $\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{can}} \geq .30$ retention floor) were fixed before
the corresponding analyses were run.

The sharpest contrast in our data is between W6 and W3. Both replace content in the
problem; W6 replaces the numbers and W3 replaces the cover story. The underlying algorithm
and the difficulty class are held fixed in both.

Gemini answers $90.9\%$ of canonical items correctly, $95.8\%$ when the numbers are
regenerated, and $52.3\%$ when the entities are renamed. The procedure evidently
generalises across numeric instantiations of the same problem while remaining
sensitive to the lexical frame in which those numbers are embedded. Claude shows the
same pattern more weakly ($.750$ on both W6 and W3 against $.841$ canonical) and
o4-mini is essentially flat across both.

Two consequences follow. First, a single scalar ``robustness'' summarises very
little: the same model can be perfectly robust to one class of change and fragile to
another. Second, the dissociation localises what the sensitive representation is
keyed to. Whatever supports the correct answer is stable under substitution of the
quantities and unstable under substitution of the names.

\section{Formal Notation Costs More Than Renaming}

W4 rewrites the problem in formal notation while leaving entity names untouched. It
is consistently among the most damaging transformations, frequently exceeding W3.

On arithmetic, Gemini falls to $.477$ under W4 against $.523$ under W3 and $.909$
canonical; Claude to $.636$; and o4-mini, which is otherwise near-invariant, to
$.682$ from $.841$, a 16-point loss that no other transformation inflicts on it. On
planning the pattern is stronger still: Claude $.119$ and Gemini $.068$ under W4,
both below their W3 values.

This is not a lexical effect, since the entities are unchanged. It indicates
sensitivity to the representational register of the problem statement independent of
its content vocabulary, which the perturbation literature's focus on renaming does
not capture.

\section{Direction of Reasoning in Planning}

W5 on Blocksworld exchanges the initial and goal states. The transformed problem is
a valid planning instance of comparable structure, re-solved with an admissible
planner to obtain its gold plan. For most models it is markedly \emph{easier} than
the original.

Direction asymmetry in planning has been reported before and linked to search
complexity \citep{fwdbwd2024}. We replicate it on PlanBench Blocksworld items and
leave its mechanism open; a pre-registered discriminating test (backward-reasoning
instruction on canonical items versus constraint asymmetry per item) is specified in
our repository.

Claude moves from $.172$ to $.873$, Gemini from $.391$ to $.764$, o4-mini from
$.781$ to $.909$. The effect is large, appears in three of five models, and is
absent in the two models at the accuracy floor.

We do not have a mechanism for this. Two hypotheses are testable with the present
design: that goal states in the released instances are structurally more constrained
than initial states, so exchanging them reduces the effective branching factor; or
that forward planning from a sparse initial state requires longer unguided search
than backward chaining from a specified goal. Distinguishing them requires
generating instances with matched initial and goal complexity, which is a small
experiment.

\section{Fragility Is a Model--Item Interaction}

\paragraph{No item-level locus.} Among items evaluated on all five primary models,
the number failing for every model is $0/110$ on the optimisation family and $0/20$
on arithmetic. On planning it is $14/64$, and all fourteen lie in the obfuscated
condition. Outside obfuscated planning there is essentially no universally fragile
item. Which problems break under renaming depends on the model, not on an intrinsic
property of the problem.

\paragraph{Within-model structure.} The contingency coefficient $\phi$ between
canonical and renamed correctness is positive in most cells and varies widely:
on optimisation, Claude $.68$ $[.53, .84]$, GPT-4o $.52$ $[.33, .70]$, Gemini $.33$
$[.19, .49]$; on arithmetic, o4-mini $.66$ against Gemini $.01$ and GPT-4o $-.03$.
A high $\phi$ means the renamed problem set is roughly the canonical set with the
hardest items removed; a $\phi$ near zero means renaming produces a nearly
orthogonal set of solvable problems. Models differ in which regime they occupy.

\paragraph{Transformation difficulty is a family property.} Ranking W1--W6 by
accuracy, the ordering is significantly concordant across models within a family:
Kendall $W = .419$ ($p = .049$) on optimisation, $.679$ ($p < .001$) on planning,
$.660$ ($p = .042$) on arithmetic. Across families within a model it is not:
Claude $W = .165$ ($p = .824$), Gemini $.238$ ($p = .673$). ``Which surface
transform is hardest'' is therefore a property of the task family, and statements of
the form ``model $X$ is rename-fragile'' are not well formed without a family index.

\paragraph{Reliable dissociations occur in arithmetic only.} Testing pairwise
model--item crossovers against an additive item-plus-model null, arithmetic yields
20 reliable crossovers against a null expectation of $0.57$ ($p = .001$), while
optimisation and planning are at or below chance. Only in arithmetic is there
behavioural evidence for genuinely separable competencies rather than a single
ability axis with noise.

\paragraph{Variance loads on different facets by family.} A crossed variance
decomposition of binary accuracy places $.327$ of optimisation variance on the model
facet against $.018$ on items ($G_{\mathrm{item}} = .415$), and inverts on
arithmetic: items $.186$, model $.009$, $G_{\mathrm{item}} = .680$. Arithmetic items
differ meaningfully from one another; optimisation items largely do not, and what
varies is the model. The two families consequently require different experimental
designs, and fitting a latent-strategy mixture confirms this: on arithmetic, BIC
selects a single class over $K \in \{1,\dots,4\}$, so no discrete strategy
subpopulations are recovered at this sample size, while optimisation and planning
fall below the item-variance threshold for such a model to be identifiable at all.

\section{Robustness Is a Capacity Distinct From Accuracy}

Surface invariance and procedural consistency are measured here by probes that share
no machinery: one compares accuracy across rewritten problem statements, the other
compares a declared plan against its execution in an isolated session.

The two probes agree ($r = .243$, $p = .024$), and neither tracks canonical
accuracy: the rank correlation between accuracy and rename retention is $.136$
($p = .642$), and between accuracy and $\phi$ it is $-.426$ ($p = .146$). Together
these give convergent and discriminant validity for robustness as a distinct
capacity.

The effect is not detectable within either family alone. At the per-family sample
sizes typical of this literature, a real association of this magnitude is invisible,
which bears on how such studies should be powered.

\paragraph{Mean consistency by model.} On arithmetic, Gemini attains the highest
plan--execution consistency ($.270$), followed by Claude ($.231$), o4-mini ($.220$),
Llama ($.167$) and GPT-4o ($.108$). On optimisation, Claude leads at $.384$,
GPT-4o $.317$, Llama $.214$. High consistency does not imply high accuracy: GPT-4o
has the lowest arithmetic consistency and a median of zero, yet answers correctly at
rates comparable to models with twice its consistency.

\section{Compliance and Correctness Are Separable}

At a sampled interior step we replace the true intermediate value with a plausible
incorrect one. Acceptance is high: Claude $88.5\%$, GPT-4o $93.4\%$, o4-mini
$100\%$, Llama $39.3\%$. Gemini is the outlier, treating every injection as
malformed input and proceeding without it ($0\%$ compliance).

Final-answer accuracy is nonetheless close to the uninjected baseline: Claude $.525$
against $.541$, GPT-4o $.508$ against $.557$, o4-mini $.377$ against $.409$. The
injected value propagates into many subsequent steps, yet the final answer survives.

Models therefore adopt a false premise, carry it forward visibly, and still reach the
correct result at near-baseline rates. Any monitoring scheme that treats the
intermediate trace as a faithful record of the computation producing the answer must
account for this.

\paragraph{A signature of reasoning training.} Among rename errors we score
\emph{intrusion}: answers that reuse a token from the original surface form after it
has been renamed. On optimisation, o4-mini intrudes at $.619$ (13 of 21 errors),
against Claude $.250$, Gemini $.143$, GPT-4o $.054$ and Llama $.000$. On planning
the rate is zero for every model. The reasoning-trained model is distinctively prone
to surfacing the pre-rename entity, which is a behavioural signature worth
mechanistic follow-up rather than a claim in itself.

\section{Programme and Next Experiments}

This work establishes the behavioural layer of a staged programme. Each next stage
has a stated prediction and decision criterion.

\paragraph{Mechanism.} If lexical and numeric robustness dissociate behaviourally
(Section~4), the representational correlate should be a rename-induced shift in the
accessibility of the answer token that predicts per-item rename survival within a
model. Our open-weight pilot cannot test this: on the current item set the available
open models solve too few renamed items ($1/60$ and $0/61$) to support a
within-model correlation. The experiment requires either easier items or stronger
open backbones, run unquantised so that cross-model contrasts are not confounded by
precision.

\paragraph{Direction of reasoning.} Generating Blocksworld instances with matched
initial- and goal-state complexity distinguishes the branching-factor and
search-direction accounts of Section~6. Under 100 inference calls.

\paragraph{Exposure.} Corpus membership is not recoverable for closed-weight models,
so exposure claims require open-corpus models. The planned calibration fits
behavioural indicators against known membership on such a model, with the
pre-registered criterion that indicators must track exposure at AUC $\ge 0.75$ to be
retained.

\paragraph{Training conditions and development.} Two further stages follow:
characterising the training-data conditions under which invariance forms, which
requires controlled training runs; and tracking when invariance emerges relative to
accuracy across public checkpoint series.

\section{Limitations}

The instrument is designed for per-instance diagnosis, but we do not issue
per-instance retrieval or computation labels: across a 270-configuration threshold
sweep the retrieval-consistent count ranges from 0 to 170 of 440, and mixture Rasch
models select a single class on arithmetic. Per-instance verdicts may also be
sensitive to the inference stack (batch size, kernel, accumulation precision) even at
temperature zero; we quantify this floor on open-weight models in follow-up work.

\section{Conclusion}

Perturbing a problem's surface reveals structure that accuracy hides, but the
structure is finer than a single drop. Numeric regeneration is nearly free while
entity renaming is expensive on the same algorithm; formal-notation rewriting, which
changes no names, is often costlier than renaming; and inverting a planning problem's
direction makes it easier for most models. Which transformation is hardest is a
property of the task family rather than the model, and no item is universally
fragile outside obfuscated planning. Two independent probes converge on a robustness
capacity that is uncorrelated with benchmark accuracy. Finally, models accept
injected false premises at rates up to $100\%$ and still recover the correct answer,
separating compliance from correctness. Reporting a single accuracy number, or a
single robustness number, discards all of it.
